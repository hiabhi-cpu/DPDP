2. PROBLEM STATEMENT
================================================================================

Under the DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025:

  a) Every hospital is a "Data Fiduciary" — legally responsible for patient data.

  b) Explicit, informed, purpose-specific DIGITAL consent is required before
     collecting any patient personal data. Paper forms are no longer valid.

  c) Patients must be able to withdraw consent, access their data, correct it,
     and request deletion. These rights must have a working mechanism.

  d) Any data breach must be reported to DPBI within 72 hours.

  e) A 7-year audit trail must be maintained proving consent was obtained for
     every data access event. This is the legal shield against Rs. 250 Crore fines.

  f) Consent notices must be provided in the patient's preferred language
     (22 official Indian languages are the legal standard).

  g) A Data Protection Officer (DPO) must be appointed per hospital.

Current reality:
  - 65%+ hospitals use paper consent forms (legally invalid from May 2027)
  - No HMS vendor has built a DPDP-compliant consent module
  - No affordable, hospital-specific consent software exists for SMB hospitals
  - Official DPDP Consent Manager registration does not open until Nov 2026
  - Hospitals can buy compliance software immediately — no registration required

3. LEGAL & REGULATORY POSITION
================================================================================

WHAT WE ARE: Software Vendor / Data Sub-Processor
--------------------------------------------------
We build and sell software. Hospitals use our software to manage their own
consent processes. We process consent records on behalf of hospitals, under
their instruction and their Data Fiduciary responsibility.

This is identical in legal structure to:
  - A law firm buying case management software
  - A bank using a CRM tool for customer data
  - A hospital using any SaaS HMS

Under this model:
  - We DO NOT need to register with DPBI as a Consent Manager
  - We DO NOT need Rs. 2 Crore minimum net worth to operate
  - We CAN sell and deploy our software starting today
  - We ARE subject to Data Processor obligations under DPDP — we must
    maintain security standards, audit trails, and act under hospital instruction

WHAT IS A "CONSENT MANAGER" UNDER DPDP RULE 4:
-----------------------------------------------
A Consent Manager is a regulated intermediary that acts as a CENTRALISED HUB
where a patient logs in ONCE and manages consent across ALL their hospitals,
banks, insurers, and data fiduciaries from a single interface.

This requires:
  - Registration with DPBI (opens November 13, 2026)
  - India incorporation
  - Rs. 2 Crore minimum net worth
  - Technical and operational capacity review by DPBI

We are NOT this. We are a per-hospital software tool.

THE KEY LEGAL DISTINCTION:
  Consent Manager   : "Rahul logs in to our app and sees ALL hospitals where
                       he has data — Apollo, Lilavati, Wockhardt."
  Our Software      : "Rahul visits Hospital A's privacy portal and manages
                       only Hospital A's consent."

As long as we enforce this distinction in our product architecture, we are
a Software Vendor — not a Consent Manager intermediary.

OUR CONTRACTUAL RELATIONSHIP:
  - We sign a Data Processing Agreement (DPA) with each hospital
  - The DPA states we process consent data under hospital instruction
  - We maintain the required security safeguards per Rule 6
  - We provide audit logs to the hospital — they are responsible for DPBI

4. WHAT WE ARE NOT BUILDING — RED LINES
================================================================================

These features would legally reclassify us as a Consent Manager intermediary,
triggering the Rs. 2 Crore registration requirement. DO NOT BUILD THESE.

RED LINE 1 — Unified patient portal across hospitals
  FORBIDDEN: A patient logs in to "consentmanager.in" and sees:
    "You have active consent at Apollo Hospital, Lilavati, Wockhardt."
  ALLOWED: A patient logs in to "lilavati.consentmanager.in" and sees
    only Lilavati Hospital's consent records.

RED LINE 2 — Cross-hospital patient identity linking
  FORBIDDEN: Our system recognises that the patient who visited Hospital A
    is the SAME PERSON who visited Hospital B and links their records.
  ALLOWED: Hospital A's patient record and Hospital B's patient record
    are completely separate entities in our system — even if same mobile number.

RED LINE 3 — Cross-hospital visibility for any party
  FORBIDDEN: Hospital A admin can see that their patient also has consents
    at Hospital B through our dashboard.
  ALLOWED: Hospital A admin sees ONLY Hospital A's consent data.

RED LINE 4 — Our branding on the patient-facing interface
  FORBIDDEN: Patient sees "ConsentManager by [Your Startup]" as the primary
    identity when managing consent.
  ALLOWED: Patient sees "Apollo Hospital Privacy Portal — Powered by [Your Startup]"
    or just "Apollo Hospital Privacy Portal" with our logo in footer only.

RED LINE 5 — Aggregated analytics across hospitals
  FORBIDDEN: We publish / offer a dashboard showing "Average consent rates
    across all our hospital customers."
  ALLOWED: Each hospital sees only their own aggregate statistics.

ENFORCEMENT IN CODE:
  - The hospital-specific hash (Section 7 below) enforces Red Lines 2 and 3
    at the cryptographic level — cross-hospital matching is mathematically
    impossible without the other hospital's key.
  - Per-hospital subdomain or custom domain enforces Red Line 1 and 4.
  - PostgreSQL Row-Level Security scoped to hospital_id enforces Red Line 3.

5. PRODUCT SCOPE — STEP 1 ONLY
================================================================================

STEP 1 — DPDP Consent Management Software (This Document)
----------------------------------------------------------
Build a standalone per-hospital consent management platform that:

  1. Captures DPDP-compliant digital consent from patients at OPD/IPD
  2. Stores consent records in an immutable, encrypted per-hospital audit vault
  3. Integrates with existing HMS via webhooks and REST API
  4. Provides dashboards for: DPO, Hospital Admin, Doctor (badge), Patient
  5. Handles: consent withdrawal, emergency access, breach notifications
  6. Supports 22 Indian languages for consent notices
  7. Works offline (tablet kiosk for hospitals with poor internet)
  8. Patient portal is hospital-branded, per-hospital URL

STEP 2 — ABDM Integration (REMOVED / DEFERRED INDEFINITELY)
------------------------------------------------------------
ABDM integration was originally planned to enable cross-hospital health record
sharing. This is removed because:

  a) Our data silo architecture deliberately prevents cross-hospital patient
     recognition — which is the prerequisite for ABDM M2 record sharing.

  b) Enabling ABDM M2 would require us to link the same patient across
     Hospital A and Hospital B — which would cross the Red Line 2 boundary
     and require Consent Manager registration.

  c) ABDM integration can be revisited ONLY IF:
     - We obtain DPDP Consent Manager registration (post Nov 2026)
     - We reach Rs. 2 Crore net worth threshold
     - The product strategy shifts to a full Consent Manager platform

For now: Step 1 provides 100% DPDP compliance for hospitals without ABDM.
Hospitals that want ABDM record sharing can handle that through their HMS
vendor's ABDM module — it is outside our product scope.

6. CORE CONCEPTS & DEFINITIONS
================================================================================

Data Fiduciary     : The hospital. They decide why and how patient data is used.
                     They own the full legal liability for patient data under DPDP.

Data Processor     : Us. We process consent records on behalf of hospitals, under
                     their instruction. We are a software vendor / sub-processor.

Data Principal     : The patient. The person whose data is being collected.

Consent Artifact   : A cryptographic record proving consent was given.
                     Contains: patient hash, hospital ID, purpose, timestamp,
                     SHA-256 hash of all fields. Immutable once written.

Consent Vault      : Our encrypted, append-only database storing consent artifacts.
                     Rows can NEVER be updated or deleted. Only new rows added.
                     Each hospital's vault is logically and cryptographically
                     isolated from every other hospital.

Patient Key        : HMAC_SHA256(mobile + SYSTEM_SALT + hospital_specific_key)
                     This is the patient's identity within ONE hospital's context.
                     The SAME patient at a different hospital produces a COMPLETELY
                     DIFFERENT hash — enforcing data silo at cryptographic level.

SYSTEM_SALT        : A secret random string stored in AWS Secrets Manager.
                     Prevents rainbow table attacks on the hash.
                     Never changes. Never exposed.

hospital_specific_key : A unique secret key per hospital, stored in AWS Secrets
                     Manager. Generated at hospital onboarding. Never exposed.
                     Ensures Hospital A's patient keys cannot be computed by
                     anyone who doesn't have Hospital A's specific key.

Audit Log          : Append-only log of every API event — consent checks, data
                     accesses, emergency overrides, withdrawals. Legal evidence.
                     Each hospital's audit log is isolated from others.

DPO                : Data Protection Officer. Appointed by the HOSPITAL internally.
                     Uses our DPO dashboard to manage their hospital's compliance.
                     NOTE: This is NOT a government officer. It is the hospital's
                     own compliance appointee.

DPBI               : Data Protection Board of India. The government enforcement
                     body. They receive breach reports from hospitals. They do NOT
                     login to our system — we provide audit exports to hospitals
                     who provide them to DPBI on request.

Bypass Event       : A doctor accesses patient data in HMS WITHOUT calling our
                     consent check API. Detected within the SAME hospital by
                     cross-referencing that hospital's HMS access log with our
                     consent check log. No cross-hospital comparison ever.

Hospital Portal    : A patient-facing URL branded as the hospital's own portal.
                     e.g. apollo-bandra.consentmanager.in OR privacy.apollobandra.com
                     Patients see the hospital's name/logo, not our startup name.

7. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

HIGH-LEVEL LAYERS:

  [Patient Touchpoints — per hospital, hospital branded]
    - OPD tablet kiosk (Android app — offline capable)
    - Hospital-branded patient portal (subdomain or custom domain)
    - SMS/WhatsApp consent link (hospital name in message)

  [API Gateway]
    - Rate limiting (per hospital API key)
    - JWT authentication (hospital-scoped tokens)
    - mTLS for HMS-to-our-system webhooks
    - Request/response logging
    - Hospital identity enforced on EVERY request

  [Core Go Services]
    - consent-service    : capture, check, store consent
    - audit-service      : append-only event logging
    - withdrawal-service : process consent withdrawals
    - emergency-service  : Section 7(b) emergency overrides
    - notification-service: OTP, WhatsApp, SMS, email
    - report-service     : per-hospital compliance reports
    - auth-service       : JWT, API keys, RBAC, hospital isolation
    - integration-service: HMS webhook adapters

  [Data Layer — all scoped by hospital_id]
    - PostgreSQL — consent vault (append-only, RLS by hospital_id)
    - PostgreSQL — audit log (append-only, RLS by hospital_id)
    - Redis — OTP store, session cache, job queue
    - AWS S3 India — consent PDFs (per-hospital bucket prefix)
    - AWS RDS Mumbai (ap-south-1 ONLY — data localization mandate)

  [External Integrations — per hospital]
    - Hospital HMS (Bahmni, eHospital, Practo, generic — webhooks/REST)
    - MSG91 / Exotel (SMS OTP)
    - WhatsApp Business API (hospital-named sender)
    - AWS SES (email — hospital branded templates)
    - Razorpay (billing)

CRITICAL ARCHITECTURE RULES:
  1. All AWS resources MUST be in ap-south-1 Mumbai — DPDP data localisation
  2. Every DB table MUST have hospital_id — enforced by PostgreSQL RLS
  3. Patient mobile NEVER stored — only HMAC_SHA256(mobile+SYSTEM_SALT+hosp_key)
  4. Audit log: PostgreSQL RLS blocks UPDATE/DELETE at DB level, not app level
  5. Consent vault: append-only — application-level AND DB-level enforcement
  6. All API calls: HTTPS TLS 1.3 minimum
  7. Emergency access: ALWAYS returns allowed:true — never block emergency
  8. Cross-hospital queries: MUST NOT EXIST anywhere in the codebase
  9. Hospital keys: stored in AWS Secrets Manager, never in code or env vars

HOSPITAL DATA ISOLATION — HOW IT WORKS:

  Hospital A onboarding:
    hospital_key_A = random_256_bit_secret() → stored in AWS Secrets Manager
    patient_key = HMAC_SHA256("9820XXXXXX" + SYSTEM_SALT + hospital_key_A)
    Result: "a3f9c2e1d7b8..."

  Same patient at Hospital B:
    hospital_key_B = different random_256_bit_secret()
    patient_key = HMAC_SHA256("9820XXXXXX" + SYSTEM_SALT + hospital_key_B)
    Result: "f7e2a1c9d4b3..." ← completely different hash

  Since Hospital B's key is never known to Hospital A (or to us without
  looking up Secrets Manager), it is cryptographically impossible to:
    - Determine that these two hashes belong to the same patient
    - Query one hospital's vault to find records from another hospital
    - Build a cross-hospital patient profile

  This is the technical enforcement of our legal data silo commitment.

8. DETAILED FLOW — PATIENT AT HOSPITAL A (NEW PATIENT)
================================================================================

Step 1: Patient arrives at OPD
  - Patient walks in. Gives name and mobile to front desk.
  - Front desk uses their existing HMS normally — zero change in workflow.

Step 2: HMS registers patient
  - HMS creates patient record: local ID "PA-00234"
  - HMS fires webhook to our API:

    POST https://api.consentmanager.in/v1/webhook/patient-registered
    Headers:
      X-Hospital-API-Key: <hospital_A_api_key>
      X-Timestamp: <unix_timestamp>
      X-Signature: HMAC_SHA256(request_body + api_key) // webhook authenticity
    Body:
    {
      "patientId": "PA-00234",
      "mobile": "9820XXXXXX",
      "hospitalId": "H-MUMBAI-001",
      "timestamp": "2026-04-12T09:30:00Z"
    }

  NOTE: If HMS does not support webhooks (older systems), the tablet kiosk
  accepts direct patient mobile entry — webhook is optional.

Step 3: Our system generates patient key for Hospital A
  - Load hospital_key_A from AWS Secrets Manager
  - Compute: patientKey = HMAC_SHA256("9820XXXXXX" + SYSTEM_SALT + hospital_key_A)
  - Query: SELECT id FROM consent_vault
           WHERE patient_key = ? AND hospital_id = 'H-MUMBAI-001'
           AND status = 'ACTIVE'
  - Result: No existing consent → proceed to consent capture
  - The mobile number "9820XXXXXX" is DISCARDED after hashing. Never stored.

Step 4: Tablet kiosk shows DPDP consent form
  - Language auto-detected from device locale or patient selects from list
  - Form shows consent notice (standalone, plain language — DPDP Rule 3):

    --- CONSENT NOTICE ---
    [Hospital A Logo]
    [Hospital A Name] — Patient Consent

    We want to collect the following information about you:
      • Your name, age, contact details, address
      • Your medical history, diagnoses, prescriptions
      • Lab reports and investigation results

    Why we collect this:
      [ ✓ ] For your treatment and care (Required — cannot be declined)
      [ _ ] To process insurance / TPA claims (Optional)
      [ _ ] For anonymous medical research (Optional)

    Who may see your data:
      • Doctors and nurses treating you at [Hospital A]
      • Our diagnostic labs (for test processing)
      • Your insurance company (only if you consent above)

    Your rights:
      • Withdraw consent anytime: SMS WITHDRAW to XXXXX
      • View your consents: [hospital-portal-URL]
      • Request data correction or deletion via the portal

    An OTP will be sent to your registered mobile to confirm your consent.
    --- END OF NOTICE ---

  - OTP sent to patient mobile (5-minute expiry, 3-attempt limit)

Step 5: Patient confirms consent via OTP
  - Patient enters OTP on tablet
  - If OTP fails 3 times:
    → Flag for front desk manual verification
    → Staff member enters their staff ID to authorise manual consent
    → Consent record created with otp_verified: false, staff_override: "STAFF-ID"
  - On success: consent artifact created

Step 6: Consent artifact written to vault
  {
    id:              UUID v4
    hospital_id:     "H-MUMBAI-001"
    patient_key:     HMAC_SHA256(mobile + SYSTEM_SALT + hospital_key_A)
    hms_patient_id:  "PA-00234"
    type:            "CONSENT_GIVEN"
    purposes:        ["treatment", "insurance"]
    language:        "marathi"
    notice_version:  "v1.2"  // version of consent notice shown
    otp_verified:    true
    timestamp:       "2026-04-12T09:34:00Z"
    kiosk_id:        "KIOSK-OPD-001"
    status:          "ACTIVE"
    hash:            SHA256(all above fields concatenated)
    version:         1
  }

  IMPORTANT: This record is IMMUTABLE. It can never be updated or deleted.
  Any change creates a NEW record with version incremented and previous_id set.

  After writing:
  - Consent status pushed back to HMS:
    POST [hospital_webhook_url]/consent-status
    Body: { "patientId": "PA-00234", "status": "ACTIVE",
            "purposes": ["treatment", "insurance"] }
  - Doctor's HMS screen: green consent badge appears next to patient name
  - Audit log entry: CONSENT_GRANTED (hospital_id, patient_key, purposes, timestamp)
  - Patient SMS: "Your consent for treatment at [Hospital A] is confirmed.
    To manage: [hospital-portal-URL]"

9. DETAILED FLOW — PATIENT AT HOSPITAL A (RETURNING PATIENT — SAME HOSPITAL)
================================================================================

This is a patient returning to the SAME Hospital A for a follow-up visit.

Step 1: Patient arrives, gives mobile at front desk
Step 2: HMS fires webhook with same mobile number

Step 3: Our system finds existing consent
  - Same patientKey computed (same hospital_key_A)
  - Query finds ACTIVE consent record
  - Response to HMS: consent is already active

Step 4: Consent check at doctor access
  - When doctor opens patient record:
    GET /v1/consent/check?patientId=PA-00234&hospitalId=H-MUMBAI-001&purpose=treatment
    Response: { "allowed": true, "consentId": "uuid-xxx", "purposes": [...] }
  - Existing consent is valid — no new consent capture needed

Step 5: When to re-capture consent
  Consent re-capture is needed when:
    a) Consent has been withdrawn by patient (status = WITHDRAWN)
    b) Hospital requests a new purpose not in original consent
       (e.g., patient originally consented to treatment only, now needs
       to add insurance consent for a new claim)
    c) Hospital's consent notice version changed significantly (legal update)
    d) Patient explicitly requests a consent update

  Re-consent flow: same as new patient flow (Step 4–6 above)
  New version created, previous_id links to original record

10. DETAILED FLOW — PATIENT AT HOSPITAL B (NEW ENTITY — DIFFERENT DATA)
================================================================================

ARCHITECTURAL DECISION: The same patient visiting Hospital B is treated as
an ENTIRELY NEW ENTITY in our system. This is not an oversight — it is the
deliberate legal design that keeps us as a Software Vendor, not a Consent Manager.

There is NO mechanism in our system to:
  - Check if this patient visited Hospital A
  - Import or reference Hospital A's consent
  - Notify Hospital A that their patient is now at Hospital B

The flow at Hospital B is IDENTICAL to Step 8 (New Patient at Hospital A)
with Hospital B's own API key, Hospital B's own hospital_key, and Hospital B's
own patient portal URL.

Hospital B gets:
  - Their own API key
  - Their own hospital_specific_key (in Secrets Manager)
  - Their own patient portal: [hospitalB-name].consentmanager.in
  - Their own consent vault partition (hospital_id = H-MUMBAI-002)
  - Their own audit log partition

Patient experience at Hospital B:
  - Patient fills consent form as if for the first time
  - Sees "Hospital B" branding throughout
  - Gets Hospital B's portal URL
  - OTP verification is independent

The patient's consent at Hospital A is completely invisible to Hospital B.
The patient's consent at Hospital B is completely invisible to Hospital A.

This is enforced at three independent layers:
  Layer 1: Cryptographic — different hash for same mobile at different hospitals
  Layer 2: Database — PostgreSQL RLS isolates rows by hospital_id
  Layer 3: Application — all queries include hospital_id from JWT claim

11. DETAILED FLOW — DOCTOR ACCESS
================================================================================

Step 1: Doctor logs into their existing HMS (no change to HMS login)

Step 2: Doctor opens a patient record
  - HMS calls our consent check API:
    GET /v1/consent/check
    Headers: Authorization: Bearer <hospital_jwt>  // hospital_id extracted here
    Query: patientId=PA-00234, purpose=treatment
  - Our system: extract hospital_id from JWT → compute patient_key →
    check consent_vault WHERE patient_key = ? AND hospital_id = ? AND status = ACTIVE
  - NOTE: hospital_id from JWT, not from request query — prevents spoofing

  Response A — Consent active:
    { "allowed": true, "consentId": "uuid-xxx",
      "purposes": ["treatment", "insurance"], "capturedAt": "..." }
    → HMS displays patient data normally
    → Audit log: DATA_ACCESSED (doctor_id, patient_key, purpose, timestamp)

  Response B — No consent:
    { "allowed": false, "reason": "no_consent",
      "action": "Please direct patient to front desk for consent capture" }
    → HMS shows warning (via embedded JS widget or HMS's own logic)
    → Audit log: CONSENT_MISSING_ACCESS_ATTEMPT (doctor_id, patient_key, timestamp)

  Response C — Consent withdrawn:
    { "allowed": false, "reason": "consent_withdrawn",
      "withdrawnAt": "...", "purposes_withdrawn": ["insurance"] }

Step 3: Bypass detection (background — within same hospital only)
  - Nightly job for each hospital separately:
    Compare: HMS access events for hospital H vs consent check API calls for H
  - If HMS logged 150 patient record opens but only 120 consent checks:
    → 30 bypass events flagged for that hospital
    → Report sent to Hospital H's DPO and admin
    → Logged as BYPASS_DETECTED in Hospital H's audit log
  - This job NEVER compares across hospitals — each hospital processed in isolation

12. DETAILED FLOW — EMERGENCY ACCESS
================================================================================

LEGAL BASIS: DPDP Act Section 7(b) — Deemed Consent for Emergency

Emergency access is always permitted but NEVER uncaptured. Every emergency
access creates an immutable audit record immediately.

Step 1: Patient arrives in emergency
  - Doctor initiates emergency override in HMS or kiosk
  - The following fields are MANDATORY (cannot be submitted blank):
    a) Staff ID (auto-populated from HMS login, or manually entered)
    b) Emergency reason:
         [ ] Patient is unconscious / unresponsive
         [ ] Life-threatening condition requiring immediate access
         [ ] Patient is a minor — guardian unavailable
         [ ] Patient is mentally incapacitated
    c) Brief clinical note (free text, max 200 characters)

Step 2: Emergency API call
  POST /v1/consent/emergency-override
  Headers: Authorization: Bearer <hospital_jwt>
  Body:
  {
    "hmsPatientId": "PA-00234",       // if known
    "mobile": "9820XXXXXX",            // if known (hashed immediately)
    "doctorId": "DR-456",
    "emergencyReason": "unconscious",
    "clinicalNote": "RTA, trauma bay",
    "timestamp": "2026-04-12T03:14:00Z"
  }

  RESPONSE: ALWAYS { "allowed": true, "emergencyId": "EMRG-2026-0042" }
  Maximum response time: 300ms. We NEVER block emergency access.

Step 3: Emergency record created immediately
  {
    id:                  UUID
    hospital_id:         "H-MUMBAI-001"
    patient_key:         HMAC_SHA256(mobile + SYSTEM_SALT + hosp_key) if mobile known
                         OR null if patient identity unknown
    hms_patient_id:      "PA-00234" or null
    type:                "EMERGENCY_OVERRIDE"
    legal_basis:         "DPDP_SECTION_7B"
    doctor_id:           "DR-456"
    emergency_reason:    "unconscious"
    clinical_note:       "RTA, trauma bay"
    status:              "PENDING_DPO_REVIEW"
    dpo_deadline:        timestamp + 72 hours
    timestamp:           now
    patient_notified:    false
  }
  Audit log: EMERGENCY_ACCESS

Step 4: DPO review within 72 hours
  - DPO dashboard shows red alert: "1 emergency access pending review"
  - DPO reviews clinical note and marks:
    [ ] VERIFIED_EMERGENCY — legitimate, record closed
    [ ] REQUIRES_INVESTIGATION — possible misuse, escalate
  - If unreviewed after 72 hours:
    → Auto-escalation email to hospital admin
    → Status changes to OVERDUE_DPO_REVIEW
    → Flagged in monthly compliance report

Step 5: Patient notification (when stable)
  - Once patient is contactable, automated SMS:
    "Your medical records at [Hospital A] were accessed on [date] under
    emergency conditions by Dr. [name]. Reason: [reason]. Manage your
    consent at: [hospital-portal-URL]"
  - patient_notified updated to true in record

Step 6: Retrospective consent
  - Patient invited to formally consent via portal or front desk
  - If patient consents: new CONSENT_GIVEN record created, linked to emergency record
  - If patient declines or raises grievance: DPO notified, hospital must respond

13. DETAILED FLOW — CONSENT WITHDRAWAL
================================================================================

Patient can withdraw consent via:
  a) Hospital's patient portal (per-hospital URL)
  b) SMS to a dedicated number
  c) WhatsApp message
  d) Front desk at the hospital

Step 1: Patient initiates withdrawal
  - Patient visits hospital's portal (e.g., apollo-bandra.consentmanager.in)
  - Or SMS: "WITHDRAW INSURANCE" to [number]

Step 2: Identity verification
  - OTP sent to patient mobile
  - Patient_key computed using that hospital's key for verification

Step 3: Withdrawal record written (append-only, never modifies original)
  {
    id:            new UUID
    hospital_id:   "H-MUMBAI-001"
    patient_key:   same hash as original
    type:          "WITHDRAWAL"
    purposes:      ["insurance"]  // can withdraw specific purposes
    status:        "WITHDRAWN"
    previous_id:   UUID of most recent active consent record
    timestamp:     now
    hash:          SHA256(all fields)
  }
  Original record remains untouched — it is the legal evidence of original consent.

Step 4: Propagate to HMS
  POST [hospital_webhook_url]/consent-status
  Body: { "patientId": "PA-00234", "status": "WITHDRAWN",
          "purposes": ["insurance"], "effectiveFrom": timestamp }

Step 5: Patient confirmation
  - SMS: "Your insurance consent at [Hospital A] has been withdrawn.
    Your treatment consent remains active. Manage: [hospital-portal-URL]"

IMPORTANT:
  - Treatment consent withdrawal does not stop emergency care
  - Insurance consent withdrawal stops future TPA sharing only
  - Existing clinical records are NOT deleted — only future sharing stops
  - Patient can re-consent at any time

Hospitals must notify DPBI within 72 hours of a breach. We support this.

Step 1: Breach detected / reported

  - Our system logs: DPBI_NOTIFICATION_SUBMITTED event

Step 5: Patient notification
  - We send bulk notification to affected patients (identified by consent vault)
  - Patients receive SMS/WhatsApp with breach information
  - Hospital can review notification content before sending

Step 6: Post-breach audit trail
  - All steps logged in audit trail
  - Breach record available for DPBI investigation on request as PDF export

# General Guide: Cryptographic Key Rotation & Migration Strategies

This guide outlines how to manage secret keys used for sensitive data hashing (HMAC), focusing on how to transition from old keys to new ones without losing data access or compromising security.

---

## 1. Lazy Migration (Routine Rotation)

**Scenario:** Standard security hygiene where keys are rotated on a schedule (e.g., annually). The old key is still secure and trusted; the goal is simply to limit the "lifetime" of any single key.

**The State:**
* **Active Key (Latest):** Used for all new incoming data.
* **Retired Key (Previous):** Still trusted, used to verify existing legacy data.

**The Workflow:**
1. **User Interaction:** A user provides their raw identifier (e.g., Phone Number or Email).
2. **Database Lookup:** The system finds a record hashed with the Retired Key (identified by a version prefix like `v1|`).
3. **Verification:** The system computes the HMAC using the Retired Key. If it matches the stored hash, the user is authenticated.
4. **The "Lazy" Upgrade:** Since the raw identifier is currently in memory during the login process, the system immediately computes a new HMAC using the Active Key.
5. **Database Update:** The system replaces the `v1` hash with the new `v2` hash.
6. **Result:** Over time, active users are automatically migrated to the newest security standard with zero friction or downtime.

---

## 2. Key Compromise (Emergency Rotation)

**Scenario:** A specific key version is confirmed to be stolen or leaked. You can no longer trust that a matching hash proves the identity of the user, as a hacker could have generated that hash themselves.

**The State:**
* **Compromised Key:** Marked as "Untrusted."
* **New Secure Key:** The fresh replacement key.

**The Workflow:**
1. **User Interaction:** The user provides their raw identifier.
2. **Database Lookup:** The system identifies a record hashed with the Compromised Key.
3. **The Security Gate:** Because the key is untrusted, the system refuses to authenticate based on the hash alone.
4. **Out-of-Band Verification:** The system triggers a secondary verification (e.g., SMS OTP, Email verification, or Biometric check).
5. **Re-establishing Trust:** Once the user proves ownership via the second factor, the system knows this specific user is legitimate.
6. **The "Heal":** The system computes a new hash using the New Secure Key and overwrites the compromised record.
7. **Result:** The compromised link is destroyed and replaced with a secure one.

---

## 3. Comparison Summary

| Feature              | Lazy Migration                 | Key Compromise                   |
|----------------------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Primary Trigger** | Scheduled Maintenance          | Security Breach / Leak           |
| **Old Key Trust** | High (Trusted for Auth)        | Zero (Untrusted for Auth)        |
| **Required Factors** | Primary Identifier only        | Identifier + Secondary Proof (OTP)|
| **User Impact** | None (Invisible)               | One-time friction (Verification) |
| **Logic Requirement**| Verification + Silent Update   | Revocation + MFA + Reconstruction|

---

## 4. Implementation Best Practices

### A. Versioned Hashing
Always store your hashes with a version prefix (e.g., `v1|{hash}`). This allows your application logic to immediately know which key to fetch from your vault to perform a comparison.

### B. Centralized Key Management
Store keys in a dedicated Secret Manager rather than environment variables or hardcoded strings. This allows you to update a key's status (from "Active" to "Compromised") in one place and have all application instances react immediately.

### C. The One-Way Nature of HMAC
Remember that because HMAC is one-way, you cannot rotate keys "offline" for all users at once if you do not store the raw data. You must wait for the user to return so they can provide the raw input needed to generate the new hash.
